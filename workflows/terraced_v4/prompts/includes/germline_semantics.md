# Germline interpretation boundaries

The question is whether the detected variant raises suspicion for an inherited predisposition relevant to the patient's current haematological phenotype.

`recognised germline predisposition gene` means a gene with an established inherited predisposition to the relevant haematological disorder or syndrome.

Do NOT treat a gene as a relevant germline-predisposition gene merely because:
- it is commonly somatically mutated in myeloid neoplasia;
- it occurs in CHIP or clonal haematopoiesis;
- it has a high VAF;
- constitutional variants in the gene have been described in an unrelated syndrome; or
- the gene is diagnostically or prognostically important.

A variant may enter `suspect` only when BOTH are supported:
1. the gene has an established germline-predisposition relationship relevant to the current clinical phenotype; and
2. the observed VAF is compatible with germline origin.

VAF compatibility alone must never establish germline suspicion.

Clinical phenotype or family history is assessed only AFTER molecular suspicion has been established. Family history cannot turn a non-predisposition gene into a germline-predisposition gene.

Do not invent syndrome names, phenotype, or family-history information.

If the supplied case and supplied candidate evidence do not establish the relevant germline-predisposition relationship, do not make a positive `suspect` classification from model memory alone. Use `uncertain` only for a genuine unresolved classification issue; otherwise use `not_suspect`.
